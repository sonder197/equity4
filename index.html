Market-Based Valuation: Price and Enterprise Value Multiples

18 Learning Outcome Statements across 4 modules. This reading builds directly on the Gordon growth model (Reading 18) and FCFE/FCFF models (Reading 19) — every "justified multiple" formula here is just that same DCF value, algebraically rearranged into a ratio.

Exam focus. This topic review covers the estimation of P/E, P/B, PEG, P/S, P/CF, and enterprise value/EBITDA ratios. The justified price multiple models draw heavily on the discounted dividend and free cash flow readings. You should be able to (1) estimate a justified price multiple for an individual firm from its fundamentals, and (2) apply the method of comparables to estimate a stock's relative value against a benchmark.

Two competing philosophies — hold this frame the whole reading

Method of comparables: value a stock by comparing its multiple to a benchmark (peer, industry, index). Rooted in the Law of One Price — similar assets should sell at similar multiples. Purely relative: you can only say "over/undervalued relative to X," never "worth $Y."

Method based on forecasted fundamentals: value a stock by computing what its multiple should be, using a DCF model (usually Gordon growth) rearranged algebraically. This gives an absolute "justified" multiple, rooted in DCF theory.

LOS 20.aMODULE 20.1 · P/E MULTIPLE

Method of Comparables vs. Method of Forecasted Fundamentals

Contrast the two approaches to using price multiples in valuation and explain the economic rationale behind each.

Warm-up: what is a "multiple"?

Price multiples are ratios of a stock's market price to some fundamental variable (earnings, book value, sales, cash flow). They are among the most widely used tools for valuing equities because they let an investor quickly judge whether a stock looks overvalued, undervalued, or properly valued relative to a benchmark. Enterprise value multiples relate the total value of a company — the market value of its capital from all sources — to a measure of operating earnings such as EBITDA. Momentum indicators compare a stock's price, or a company's earnings, to their own values in earlier periods.

Two philosophies, side by side
Method of Comparables
  • Values a stock using the average price multiple of similar companies (a peer, an industry, an index, or the stock's own history).
  • Economic rationale: the Law of One Price — two similar assets should sell at comparable price multiples.
  • Relative valuation method — you can only say the stock is over/undervalued relative to the benchmark, never state an absolute intrinsic value.
Method of Forecasted Fundamentals
  • Values a stock using the ratio of its DCF-model value to a fundamental variable (e.g., EPS).
  • Economic rationale: value = present value of expected future cash flows discounted at the risk-adjusted required return — exactly the logic behind every DCF model you already know.
  • Produces a justified ("fair value") multiple — an absolute benchmark derived from theory, not from the market.

💡 The two methods are not rivals you must pick between on the exam — they are two different lenses. The method of comparables asks "is this cheap relative to its peers?" The method of forecasted fundamentals asks "is this cheap relative to what a DCF model says it should be worth?" A stock can be cheap by one lens and expensive by the other.

Worked example — Method of Comparables

MK Technologies shares sell for $50. Trailing EPS is $2. The average trailing P/E for MK's industry peers is 32×.

Solution
MK's trailing P/E = 50 / 2 = 25×
MK is relatively undervalued — its trailing P/E (25×) is below the industry average (32×). Note the word "relatively": this says nothing about MK's absolute intrinsic value, only about its price relative to peers.

Worked example — Method of Forecasted Fundamentals

Comtronics shares sell for $30. Mean forecast EPS for next year is $4.00, long-run growth is 5%, the payout ratio is 60%, and the required return is 14%. Find the fundamental value using the Gordon growth model, then judge over/undervaluation.

Solution
V₀ = D₁ / (r − g) = (0.60 × 4) / (0.14 − 0.05) = 2.40 / 0.09 = $26.67
Fair-value (justified) leading P/E = 26.67 / 4.00 = 6.67×
Observed leading P/E = 30 / 4.00 = 7.5×
Comtronics is overvalued: the observed multiple (7.5×) exceeds the justified multiple (6.67×). Equivalently: market price ($30.00) > intrinsic value ($26.67) — same conclusion, two routes.

LOS 20.bMODULE 20.1 · P/E MULTIPLE

Calculate and Interpret a Justified Price Multiple

The core interpretive rule that governs every multiple in this reading.

A justified price multiple is what the multiple should be if the stock is fairly valued — derived either from the method of comparables (the benchmark's multiple) or the method of forecasted fundamentals (a DCF-based ratio, e.g., the constant-growth P/E = payout ratio / (r − g)).

The single rule that runs through the whole reading
Actual multiple > Justified multiple → stock is OVERVALUED
Actual multiple < Justified multiple → stock is UNDERVALUED
(all else, i.e. risk and growth, assumed equal between the stock and its justified benchmark)

A price multiple can be "justified" two different ways, mirroring LOS 20.a:

  • By the method of comparables: the justified multiple is simply the benchmark's current multiple (a peer's P/E, an industry median P/E, etc.).
  • By the method of forecasted fundamentals: the justified multiple is calculated from a valuation model — most commonly the Gordon growth model, e.g. justified P/E = payout ratio / (r − g).

Stocks with actual multiples below their justified multiple (by either method) are judged undervalued; stocks with actual multiples above their justified multiple are judged overvalued.

💡 "Justified" is not a synonym for "correct" — it is a benchmark. The entire rest of this reading is really about two questions: (1) how do I calculate a justified multiple for each of the five ratios (P/E, P/B, P/S, P/CF, EV/EBITDA)? and (2) how do I pick a sensible comparables benchmark? Keep coming back to this LOS as the organizing spine.

LOS 20.cMODULES 20.1–20.4

Rationales and Drawbacks of the Five Price Multiples + Dividend Yield

Describe rationales for and possible drawbacks to using alternative price multiples and dividend yield in valuation. This LOS is tested qualitatively across all 5 ratios — memorize the pattern, not just the words.

💡 Pattern to notice: every ratio's "rationale" column praises what the denominator captures; every "drawback" column complains about how easily that same denominator can be distorted, negative, or non-comparable. Learn the pattern once and each ratio becomes easy to reconstruct.

P/E Ratio

Rationale vs. drawbacks
✅ Rationale
  • Earnings power (EPS) is the primary determinant of investment value.
  • The P/E ratio is extremely popular in the investment community — easy to communicate.
⚠️ Drawbacks
  • Earnings can be negative → meaningless P/E ratio.
  • The volatile, transitory portion of earnings makes P/E interpretation difficult.
  • Management discretion within allowed accounting practices can distort reported earnings.

P/B Ratio

Rationale vs. drawbacks
✅ Rationale
  • Book value is cumulative and usually positive even when EPS is negative.
  • Book value is more stable than EPS — useful when EPS is unusually high, low, or volatile.
  • Appropriate net-asset-value measure for firms holding mostly liquid assets: finance, investment, insurance, and banking firms.
  • Useful for valuing companies expected to go out of business (liquidation-relevant).
⚠️ Drawbacks
  • Does not reflect the value of intangible economic assets such as human capital.
  • In some cases the firm's business model dictates the size of its asset base — misleading comparisons across business models.
  • Different accounting conventions across firms/countries reduce comparability.
  • Inflation and technological change can cause book and market values of assets to diverge significantly.

P/S Ratio

Rationale vs. drawbacks
✅ Rationale
  • Meaningful even for distressed firms — sales revenue is almost always positive.
  • Not as easy to manipulate or distort as EPS and book value.
  • Not as volatile as P/E multiples.
  • Particularly appropriate for valuing stocks in mature or cyclical industries and start-ups with no earnings record.
⚠️ Drawbacks
  • High sales growth does not necessarily mean high operating profit as measured by earnings and cash flow.
  • P/S ratios do not capture differences in cost structures across companies.
  • Revenue recognition practices can still distort sales forecasts.

P/CF Ratio

Rationale vs. drawbacks
✅ Rationale
  • Cash flow is harder for managers to manipulate than earnings.
  • More stable than price-to-earnings.
  • Handles the problem of differences in the quality of reported earnings.
  • Empirical evidence: differences in P/CF are significantly related to long-run average stock returns.
⚠️ Drawbacks (relate to the definition of cash flow used)
  • EPS-plus-noncash-charges ignores noncash revenue and net changes in working capital.
  • FCFE is theoretically preferable to CFO, but FCFE is more volatile than straight cash flow.

Dividend Yield (D/P)

The dividend yield is the ratio of the common dividend to the market price. It is most often used for valuing indexes.

Rationale vs. drawbacks
✅ Rationale
  • Dividend yield contributes to total investment return.
  • Dividends are not as risky as the capital appreciation component of total return.
⚠️ Drawbacks
  • Ignores capital appreciation entirely — only part of total return.
  • Implies a trade-off between current and future cash flows (high payout today can mean lower growth, hence lower future dividends).

LOS 20.dMODULES 20.1–20.4

Calculating the Five Price Multiples and Dividend Yield

Calculate and interpret alternative price multiples and dividend yield — trailing and leading variants, and the mechanics behind each ratio.

P/E Ratio — trailing vs. leading

Trailing P/E
trailing P/E = market price per share / EPS over previous 12 months
Leading (forward) P/E
leading P/E = market price per share / forecasted EPS over next 12 months
  • Trailing P/E is not useful for forecasting/valuation if the firm's business has changed materially (e.g., after a merger, divestiture, or strategy shift).
  • Leading P/E may not be relevant if earnings are sufficiently volatile that next year's EPS cannot be forecast with any accuracy.
Worked example — Byron Investments

An analyst gathers: current price €18.00, current-year net income €32,000,000, shares outstanding 40,000,000, forecasted net income next year €44,000,000 (same share count → forecasted EPS €1.00). Find trailing and leading P/E.

current-year EPS = €32,000,000 / 40,000,000 = €0.80
trailing P/E = €18.00 / €0.80 = 22.5×
leading P/E = €18.00 / €1.00 = 18.0×

P/B Ratio

Book value of equity
book value of equity = common shareholders' equity = (total assets − total liabilities) − preferred stock
P/B ratio
P/B = market value of equity / book value of equity = market price per share / book value per share

Adjustments to book value for more useful comparisons:

  • Tangible book value = book value of equity − intangible assets.
  • Adjust for significant off-balance-sheet assets and liabilities.
  • Adjust items to ensure comparability across firms (different accounting policies).
Worked example — Crisco Systems vs. Soothsayer Corp.

Crisco: book value of equity $28,039M, 7,001M shares, price $17.83. Soothsayer: book value of equity $6,320M, 5,233M shares, price $12.15.

Crisco
BVPS = $28,039 / 7,001 = $4.01   →   P/B = $17.83 / $4.01 = 4.45×
Soothsayer
BVPS = $6,320 / 5,233 = $1.21   →   P/B = $12.15 / $1.21 = 10.04×

P/S Ratio

P/S = market value of equity / total sales = market price per share / sales per share
Worked example — same Crisco / Soothsayer data, plus 20X3 sales

Crisco sales $18,878M; Soothsayer sales $9,475M (share counts as above).

Crisco
sales/share = $18,878M / 7,001M = $2.70   →   P/S = $17.83 / $2.70 = 6.60×
Soothsayer
sales/share = $9,475M / 5,233M = $1.81   →   P/S = $12.15 / $1.81 = 6.71×

P/CF Ratio

P/CF = market value of equity / cash flow = market price per share / cash flow per share
where cash flow can be defined as: CF (earnings + noncash charges), adjusted CFO, FCFE, or EBITDA — see LOS 20.n for the full comparison of these four definitions.

Dividend Yield (D/P) — trailing vs. leading

trailing D/P = (4 × most recent quarterly dividend) / market price per share
leading D/P = (forecasted dividends over next four quarters) / market price per share
Worked example — OnePrice Inc.

Price $47.50. Most recent quarterly dividend $0.50. Next four quarters forecast to be $0.50, $0.55, $0.60, $0.65.

trailing D/P = (4 × $0.50) / $47.50 = $2.00/$47.50 = 4.2%
leading D/P = ($0.50+$0.55+$0.60+$0.65) / $47.50 = $2.30/$47.50 = 4.8%

✅ Notice the mechanical symmetry: every "trailing" version uses last period's actual number; every "leading" version uses next period's forecast. This single idea — trailing vs. leading — repeats for P/E and D/P, and the same logic underlies "justified trailing" vs. "justified leading" P/E in LOS 20.i.

LOS 20.eMODULE 20.4

Underlying Earnings and Normalized EPS

Calculate and interpret underlying earnings, explain methods of normalizing EPS, and calculate normalized EPS.

Underlying earnings

Underlying earnings (also called persistent, continuing, or core earnings) exclude nonrecurring components — gains and losses from asset sales, asset write-downs, provisions for future losses, and changes in accounting estimates. Use diluted EPS as the starting point.

Rule of thumb

Nonrecurring gains inflated reported earnings → subtract them out. Nonrecurring losses/expenses depressed reported earnings → add them back. You are always solving for what earnings "really" were, absent the one-off noise.

Worked example — Magnolia Enterprises (underlying earnings)

Trailing four quarters' reported diluted EPS: Dec 2021 C$1.45, Mar 2022 C$1.30, Jun 2022 C$1.40, Sep 2022 C$1.35 (stock price at Sep 2022 = C$44.85). During the year: Mar 2022 had a C$0.30/share gain on asset sales; Jun 2022 had a C$0.55/share extraordinary expense.

12-month reported EPS = 1.45 + 1.30 + 1.40 + 1.35 = C$5.50
underlying earnings = 5.50 − 0.30 (remove gain) + 0.55 (add back expense) = C$5.75
trailing P/E (on underlying earnings) = C$44.85 / C$5.75 = 7.80×

Normalized earnings

Normalized earnings are an estimate of EPS at the middle of the business cycle — what the company would earn under mid-cycle, "normal" conditions. Two methods:

Method of historical average EPS
average EPS over the most recent full business cycle
Simple, but does not adjust for changes in firm size over the cycle.
Method of average ROE
normalized EPS = average ROE × current BVPS
Preferred — reliance on current BVPS reflects the effect of firm-size changes more accurately than the historical-average-EPS method.
Worked example — Magnolia Enterprises (normalized earnings)
Year20X120X220X320X4
EPSC$4.20C$3.75C$4.75C$4.30
BVPSC$26.02C$27.78C$29.25C$32.29
ROE14.0%12.0%16.0%14.0%
historical-average-EPS method = (4.20+3.75+4.75+4.30)/4 = C$4.25
average ROE = (0.14+0.12+0.16+0.14)/4 = 14.00%
average-ROE method = 0.14 × C$32.29 (current BVPS) = C$4.52
The Molodovsky effect

The countercyclical tendency to see high P/Es at the bottom of the earnings cycle (because EPS is temporarily depressed) and low P/Es at the top of the cycle (because EPS is temporarily inflated). This is exactly why normalized/underlying earnings are preferred to raw trailing EPS for cyclical companies — raw P/E gets systematically distorted by where the company sits in its cycle.

LOS 20.fMODULE 20.4

Earnings Yield (E/P)

Explain and justify the use of earnings yield.

The earnings yield is simply the reciprocal of the P/E ratio: E/P instead of P/E.

E/P = EPS / market price per share

Its key advantage over P/E: price is never negative, so E/P is always meaningful — even for firms with negative earnings (where P/E would be meaningless or misleading).

  • A high E/P suggests a cheap security.
  • A low E/P suggests an expensive security.

Securities can therefore be ranked from cheap to expensive based on E/P ratios — including firms with losses, where a negative E/P still ranks sensibly (a very negative E/P is clearly worse than a small positive one), unlike a negative P/E which just produces a nonsensical negative multiple.

💡 E/P also appears later in the Fed model (LOS 20.g/h territory): comparing the market's earnings yield to the 10-year Treasury yield to judge broad-market over/undervaluation.

LOS 20.gMODULE 20.4

Fundamental Factors That Influence the Multiples

Describe fundamental factors that influence alternative price multiples and dividend yield — the directional relationships you must know cold.

Every "justified multiple" formula in LOS 20.i is a rearranged Gordon growth model. That means the same few inputs — growth (g), required return (r), payout ratio, and profitability (ROE or profit margin) — drive every multiple. Master these directional relationships and the formulas in LOS 20.i become intuitive rather than memorized.

MultipleIncreases when…Decreases when…
P/Egrowth (g) increases; risk/required return (r) decreasesrisk/required return (r) increases; growth (g) decreases
P/BROE increases (wider ROE − r spread)ROE decreases (narrower or negative ROE − r spread)
P/Sprofit margin increases; earnings growth rate increasesprofit margin decreases; growth decreases
P/CFrequired return decreases; growth rate increasesrequired return increases; growth rate decreases
EV/EBITDAgrowth in FCFF and EBITDA increasesfirm's overall risk and WACC increase
Dividend yield (D/P)required return (r) increasesforecasted growth rate in dividends increases
P/B specifically — the ROE spread

Justified P/B = (ROE − g) / (r − g). P/B increases as ROE increases. The larger the spread between ROE and r, all else equal, the higher the P/B ratio — a firm earning well above its cost of equity deserves to trade at a large premium to book value.

P/S specifically — two channels for profit margin

Justified P/S = (E₀/S₀) × (1−b) × (1+g) / (r−g). Net profit margin (E₀/S₀) influences P/S directly (it's a factor in the numerator) and indirectly through its effect on the sustainable growth rate g (since g = retention ratio × ROE, and ROE embeds profit margin via DuPont).

LOS 20.hMODULE 20.4

Predicted P/E from a Cross-Sectional Regression

Calculate and interpret a predicted P/E given a cross-sectional regression on fundamentals, and explain limitations of the methodology.

A cross-sectional regression is a regression in which the explained and explanatory variables are all measured at the same point in time (e.g., across many peer firms today). A predicted P/E is estimated from a linear regression of historical P/Es on fundamental variables — typically payout ratio, growth, and risk (beta).

Worked example — public utility

An analyst is valuing a public utility with dividend payout ratio 0.50, beta 0.95, expected earnings growth 0.06. A regression on peer utilities gives:

predicted P/E = 6.75 + (4.00 × payout) + (12.35 × growth) − (0.5 × beta)

The firm's actual P/E is 12.0. Is it over- or underpriced?

predicted P/E = 6.75 + (4.00×0.50) + (12.35×0.06) − (0.5×0.95) = 9.02
Actual P/E (12.0) > predicted P/E (9.02) → the firm is overpriced.

Three limitations of the cross-sectional regression methodology

  1. The predictive power of the regression for a different time period and/or sample of stocks is uncertain.
  2. The relationships between P/E and the fundamental variables examined may change over time.
  3. Multicollinearity is often a problem in these regressions, making individual coefficients hard to interpret.
Professor's note

Multicollinearity refers to high correlation between two or more independent variables in a multiple regression — a concept from Quantitative Methods. Because payout, growth, and beta are often correlated with each other, isolating "the effect of growth alone" from the coefficient becomes unreliable even though the overall prediction may still be useful.

💡 Do not confuse this with LOS 20.i's "justified P/E" formula. The predicted P/E (this LOS) comes from an empirical regression fit to market data; the justified P/E (LOS 20.i) comes from a theoretical DCF model (Gordon growth). Both compare "actual vs. model" to judge mispricing, but the model itself is built completely differently.

LOS 20.iMODULE 20.4

Justified P/E, P/B, and P/S Based on Forecasted Fundamentals

The heart of the reading — deriving each justified multiple algebraically from the Gordon growth model.

Professor's note

The material is organized by ratio below. Once you know the justified-multiple formula for a ratio, you already know its fundamental drivers (LOS 20.g) — they're the same variables. Note that the LOS asks you to describe the fundamentals behind every multiple (including P/CF, EV/EBITDA, and dividend yield — covered in LOS 20.g), but to actually calculate only three: P/E, P/B, and P/S.

Justified P/E — derived from the Gordon growth model

Starting point: Gordon growth model
V₀ = D₀×(1+g) / (r−g) = D₁ / (r−g)
Justified trailing P/E (divide both sides by E₀; D₀/E₀ = payout ratio = 1−b)
P₀/E₀ = [D₀×(1+g)/E₀] / (r−g) = (1−b)×(1+g) / (r−g)
Justified leading P/E (divide both sides by E₁ instead)
P₀/E₁ = (D₁/E₁) / (r−g) = (1−b) / (r−g)
Professor's note — the (1+g) relationship

Since E₁ > E₀ when earnings grow, the justified leading P/E (P₀/E₁) will always be smaller than the justified trailing P/E (P₀/E₀) — you're dividing by a bigger number. In fact: justified trailing P/E = justified leading P/E × (1+g). This identity is a fast cross-check on any P/E problem.

Fundamental drivers of justified P/E:

  • Positively related to the expected growth rate of cash flows (dividends or FCF), all else equal.
  • Inversely related to the stock's required rate of return, all else equal.
  • Positively related to the inflation pass-through rate (less obvious): of two similar firms in the same inflationary environment, the one better able to pass rising costs on to customers should carry the higher P/E.
Worked example — Comtronics (cross-check against LOS 20.a)

Price $30, next-year EPS forecast $4, g=5%, payout=60%, r=14%. Justified leading P/E?

justified leading P/E = (1−0.60)/(0.14−0.05) = 0.40/0.09 = 6.67×
Identical to the "long way" answer from LOS 20.a ($26.67 fair value ÷ $4.00 = 6.67×) — same model, same answer, two routes to it.
Worked example — generic

Payout ratio 40%, required return 11%, growth 5%. Find trailing and leading P/E.

justified trailing P/E = (1−0.40)×(1.05) / (0.11−0.05) = 0.60×1.05/0.06 = 7.00×
justified leading P/E = (1−0.40) / (0.11−0.05) = 0.60/0.06 = 6.67×
Cross-check: 6.67 × 1.05 = 7.00 ✓ (matches the trailing→leading identity above)

Justified P/B — derived using g = ROE × b

Using the sustainable growth relation g = ROE × b and E₁ = B₀ × ROE, the Gordon growth model can also be rearranged in terms of book value:

justified P/B = P₀/B₀ = (ROE − g) / (r − g)
Worked example

ROE 14%, required return 8%, growth 4%.

justified P/B = (0.14−0.04)/(0.08−0.04) = 0.10/0.04 = 2.5×

Justified P/S — derived using net profit margin = E₀/S₀

Trailing sales basis
justified P₀/S₀ = (E₀/S₀) × (1−b) × (1+g) / (r−g)
Forecasted sales basis
justified P₀/S₁ = (E₁/S₁) × (1−b) / (r−g)
Useful shortcut
justified P/S = net profit margin × justified trailing P/E
Worked example

Payout 40%, ROE 8.3%, EPS $4.25, sales per share $218.75, growth 5%, required return 10%.

profit margin = E₀/S₀ = 4.25/218.75 = 0.0194
justified P/S = 0.0194 × 0.40 × 1.05 / (0.10−0.05) = 0.163×

✅ Active recall before moving on: without looking, write the three justified-multiple formulas (P/E trailing, P/B, P/S) from memory. Each is the Gordon growth model wearing a different disguise.

LOS 20.jMODULE 20.4

The P/E-to-Growth (PEG) Ratio

Calculate and interpret PEG and explain its use in relative valuation.

Justified P/E is positively related to the growth rate — so comparing raw P/E across firms with very different growth rates is misleading. The PEG ratio "standardizes" P/E by growth:

PEG ratio = (P/E ratio) / g   (g expressed as a whole number, e.g. 14.5 not 0.145)

PEG is interpreted as "P/E per unit of expected growth." The valuation rule: stocks with lower PEGs are more attractive than stocks with higher PEGs, assuming similar risk.

Worked example — Med-Ready, Inc.

Leading P/E = 28.75, 5-year consensus growth = 14.5%. Median PEG for a comparable-risk peer group = 2.34.

Med-Ready PEG = 28.75 / 14.5 = 1.98
1.98 < peer median 2.34 → Med-Ready appears undervalued. Caveat: confirm the peer PEGs also use leading P/E, and that the peers are truly comparable in risk.
Worked example — Party Favors, Inc.

Leading P/E = 18.75, growth = 15.32%. Peer median PEG (leading-P/E basis) = 0.92.

Party Favors PEG = 18.75 / 15.32 = 1.22
1.22 > peer median 0.92 → Party Favors appears overvalued.

Three drawbacks of the PEG ratio

  1. The relationship between P/E and g is not linear, which makes comparisons difficult.
  2. PEG still doesn't account for risk.
  3. PEG doesn't reflect the duration of the high-growth period in a multistage model — especially misleading if the analyst uses a short-term high-growth forecast.

LOS 20.kMODULE 20.4

Price Multiples for Terminal Value in a Multistage DCF

Calculate and explain the use of price multiples in determining terminal value in a multistage DCF model.

A terminal value at the end of the explicit forecast horizon should reflect the growth the firm can sustain over the long run beyond that point. Analysts often use terminal price multiples (P/E, P/B, P/S, P/CF) instead of a perpetuity-growth formula. Regardless of which ratio, the logic is the same:

terminal value = price multiple × fundamental variable
Based on fundamentals
= justified price multiple × forecast of the fundamental variable
Requires estimates of growth rate, required return, and payout ratio. Weakness: needs many inputs, each a source of estimation error.
Based on comparables
= benchmark price multiple × forecast of the fundamental variable
Strength: uses market data exclusively. Weakness: a benchmark that is itself mispriced transfers that error straight into your terminal value.
terminal value in year n = (justified leading P/E) × (forecasted earnings in year n+1)
terminal value in year n = (justified trailing P/E) × (forecasted earnings in year n)
Worked example — Polar Technology

EPS in Year 5 forecast = C$2.10; EPS in Year 6 forecast = C$2.32; median trailing industry P/E = 35. Find terminal value in Year 5.

terminal value (Year 5) = (benchmark trailing P/E) × (Year 5 EPS) = 35 × C$2.10 = C$73.50
Watch the trap: since we're using trailing P/E, we multiply by Year 5 earnings (C$2.10) — not the Year 6 forecast (C$2.32), which would be needed only for a leading-P/E terminal value.

LOS 20.lMETHOD OF COMPARABLES

Evaluating Over-, Fairly, or Undervalued via Comparisons of Multiples

Evaluate whether a stock is overvalued, fairly valued, or undervalued based on comparisons of multiples.

Professor's note — terminology

"Benchmark value of a multiple" is another name for the justified price multiple using the method of comparables. The reading uses "benchmark" here to match the LOS wording — don't treat it as a new concept.

The four-step benchmarking process

1
Select and calculate the multiple to be used
→
2
Select the benchmark; calculate mean/median of its multiple over the comparable group
→
3
Compare the stock's multiple to the benchmark
→
4
Check whether the difference is explained by fundamentals; make valuation adjustments

Frequently used P/E benchmarks

  • P/E of another company's stock in a similar industry with similar operating characteristics.
  • Average or median P/E of the peer group within the company's industry.
  • Average or median P/E for the industry.
  • P/E of an equity index.
  • The stock's own average historical P/E.
Worked example — Allbright Interiors vs. Basic Designs
Trailing P/ELeading P/E5-yr growthBeta
Allbright10.08.711.0%1.3
Basic Designs14.012.79.0%1.4
Peer median13.312.111.0%1.3
Allbright: P/E below the peer median (10.0 vs 13.3) despite a comparable growth rate (11.0% vs 11.0%) and beta (1.3 vs 1.3) → since the fundamentals match the benchmark but the multiple doesn't, Allbright appears genuinely undervalued.
Basic Designs: P/E above the peer median (14.0 vs 13.3) despite lower growth (9.0% vs 11.0%) and higher risk (beta 1.4 vs 1.3) — both factors that should lower its justified P/E, not raise it. This makes Basic Designs look overvalued relative to the benchmark.

💡 P/B and P/S valuation by comparables follow the exact same 4 steps, with two practical differences: (1) book-value and sales forecasts are far less widely available than EPS forecasts, so analysts typically use trailing book value / sales rather than forward estimates; (2) the fundamentals you must control for differ — ROE, risk, and growth for P/B; profit margin, growth, risk, and accounting-data quality for P/S.

Professor's note — look-ahead bias

When backtesting a screening strategy based on price multiples or other data, avoid look-ahead bias — using information that was not actually available at the historical point in time when the stocks would have been selected.

LOS 20.mMETHOD OF COMPARABLES

The Role of Fundamentals in the Method of Comparables

Evaluate a stock by the method of comparables and explain the importance of fundamentals in using it.

The basic idea: compare a stock's multiple to a benchmark. Firms with multiples below the benchmark are (tentatively) undervalued; firms above are (tentatively) overvalued. But this conclusion is only valid if the stock's fundamentals are similar to the benchmark's — you must compare apples to apples.

The three possible explanations when P/E < benchmark
1
The stock is genuinely undervalued.
2
The stock is properly valued, but has a lower expected growth rate than the benchmark — which alone justifies a lower P/E.
3
The stock is properly valued, but has a higher required return (higher risk) than the benchmark — which alone justifies a lower P/E.

Only if growth and risk are genuinely comparable to the benchmark can you conclude explanation #1 (true undervaluation) rather than #2 or #3 (properly-priced-but-different-fundamentals).

Worked example — Crisco Systems & Soothsayer vs. industry peers

Crisco (Networking Products industry): P/B 4.45, P/S 6.60. Soothsayer (Enterprise Software/Services industry): P/B 10.04, P/S 6.71.

Peer groupMean P/BMedian P/BMean P/SMedian P/S
Networking Products2.0651.1703.7330.900
Enterprise Software/Svcs7.8662.7703.3411.920
Crisco: P/B (4.45) exceeds both peer mean (2.065) and median (1.170); P/S (6.60) exceeds both peer mean (3.733) and median (0.900) → Crisco appears overvalued by both measures.
Soothsayer: P/B (10.04) exceeds both peer mean (7.866) and median (2.770); P/S (6.71) exceeds both peer mean (3.341) and median (1.920) → Soothsayer also appears overvalued by both measures.
Notice the large mean-vs-median gap in each peer group (e.g., Enterprise Software mean P/B 7.866 vs. median 2.770) — a clear sign of outliers distorting the mean.

Extending the logic to EV/EBITDA and dividend yield

  • EV/EBITDA: a lower ratio relative to peers indicates relative undervaluation; a higher ratio indicates overvaluation — same logic as P/E, P/B, P/S.
  • Dividend yield: compare the target's yield to peers (identified on the basis of comparable risk). A high yield relative to the benchmark indicates undervaluation, all else equal — but pay particular attention to whether the yield gap is really explained by a growth-rate difference instead.

LOS 20.nMODULE 20.4

Alternative Definitions of Cash Flow for P/CF and EV Multiples

Explain alternative definitions of cash flow used in price and enterprise value multiples, and describe the limitations of each.

There are (at least) four definitions of cash flow available for the P/CF ratio — expect any one of them to appear on the exam.

➕
1. Earnings-plus-noncash-charges (CF)
CF = net income + depreciation + amortization. Limitation: ignores noncash revenue and changes in net working capital.
📊
2. Adjusted cash flow (adjusted CFO)
CFO from the cash flow statement, adjusted for nonrecurring items (after-tax). Limitation: US GAAP requires interest paid/received and dividends received in CFO; IFRS is more flexible (interest paid can be operating or financing; interest/dividends received can be operating or investing) — creates cross-standard comparability issues.
💰
3. Free cash flow to equity (FCFE)
FCFE = CFO − FCInv + net borrowing. Theoretically preferred definition — but more volatile than straight cash flow.
🏭
4. EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization. A pretax, pre-interest measure — a flow to both equity and debt holders, so it's better suited as an indicator of total company value than of equity value alone (see LOS 20.o).
FCFE (full formula, from Reading 19)
FCFE = CFO − FCInv + net borrowing
FCInv = fixed capital investment; net borrowing = (LT + ST debt issues) − (LT + ST debt repayments)
P/CF = market value of equity / cash flow = market price per share / cash flow per share

Analysts typically use the trailing P/CF ratio — the most recent four quarters of cash flow per share.

Worked example — Data Management Systems (DMS)

Net income $32M, D&A $41M, net interest expense $12M, CFO $44M, tax rate 30%. 25M shares outstanding, price $47/share. Calculate P/CF using CF (earnings-plus-noncash-charges) as the proxy.

CF = $32M + $41M = $73M
market value of equity = 25M × $47 = $1,175M
P/CF = $1,175M / $73M = 16.1×
Note that net interest expense and the tax rate were not needed for this particular CF definition — a common distractor pattern in P/CF problems.

LOS 20.oMODULE 20.4

Enterprise Value Multiples and EV/EBITDA

Calculate and interpret EV multiples and evaluate the use of EV/EBITDA.

Because EBITDA is a flow to both equity and debt, it should be compared against a numerator that measures total company value, not just equity value. That numerator is enterprise value (EV).

EV = market value of common stock + market value of preferred equity + market value of debt + minority interest − cash and investments
Rationale for subtracting cash & investments: an acquirer's net price paid for a target would effectively be lowered by the amount of the target's liquid assets it acquires along with it.
EV build-up as a waterfall (Boulevard Industries numbers, Sf millions)
Market value of equity (22.50 × 40)Sf 900.0
+ Market value of debtSf 137.0
− Cash & marketable securitiesSf 62.3
− InvestmentsSf 327.0
= Enterprise ValueSf 647.7
Professor's note

Assume minority interest and preferred equity are zero unless the problem states otherwise — this is the typical exam setup. If given values for either, include them.

EV/EBITDA ratio
EV/EBITDA = enterprise value / EBITDA
enterprise value = market value of common stock + market value of debt − cash and investments (assuming no preferred/minority interest)
EBITDA = recurring earnings from continuing operations + interest + taxes + depreciation + amortization  or, for forecasting  = EBIT + depreciation + amortization
✅ EV/EBITDA is useful when…
• More useful than P/E when comparing firms with different degrees of financial leverage.
• Useful for valuing capital-intensive businesses with high D&A.
• EBITDA is usually positive even when EPS is not.
⚠️ Drawbacks
• If working capital is growing, EBITDA will overstate CFO; ignores how revenue-recognition policy affects CFO.
• FCFF captures capex, so it's more strongly linked to valuation theory than EBITDA — EBITDA is adequate only if capex ≈ depreciation.
Worked example — Boulevard Industries (Sf = Swiss francs)
Recent share priceSf 22.50
Shares outstanding40 million
Market value of debtSf 137 million
Cash & marketable securitiesSf 62.3 million
InvestmentsSf 327 million

D&ASf 10.4 million

EBITDA = 137.5+6.9+95.9+10.4 = Sf 250.7M
EV = (22.50×40) + 137 − 62.3 − 327 = Sf 647.7M
EV/EBITDA = 647.7 / 250.7 = 2.6×

Related measures

  • Total invested capital (TIC) — a.k.a. market value of invested capital — is the market value of the company's equity and debt. Unlike EV, TIC includes cash and short-term investments (it is not subtracted).
  • EV/Sales (EV/S) — an alternative to P/S, appropriate for comparing companies with significantly different capital structures.
  • Analysts also build EV ratios using EBIT or FCFF in the denominator.

LOS 20.pMODULE 20.4

Sources of Differences in Cross-Border Valuation Comparisons

Explain why comparing multiples across countries is harder than it looks.

Using relative valuation across borders is challenging because of differences in accounting methods, cultures, risk, and growth opportunities. Benchmarking itself is difficult: P/Es of individual firms in the same industry vary widely across countries, and country-level market P/Es vary significantly too.

Common categories of international accounting differences
Goodwill
Deferred income taxes
Foreign exchange adjustments
R&D treatment
Pension expense
Tangible asset revaluations

Which ratios are most/least affected?

Least affectedMost affected
Least affectedP/FCFE — cash flow is much harder to distort via accounting choices than accrual-based figures.
Most affectedP/B, P/E, P/EBITDA, EV/EBITDA — all more heavily influenced by management's choice of accounting methods and estimates.

💡 The pattern: whichever multiple's denominator is closest to actual cash is least distorted by accounting choice; whichever depends most on accrual accounting judgment (depreciation policy, goodwill treatment, pension assumptions) is most distorted.

LOS 20.qMODULE 20.4

Momentum Indicators

Describe momentum indicators and their use in valuation.

Momentum indicators relate either the market price or a fundamental variable (like EPS) to the time series of its own historical or expected value. Common momentum indicators: earnings surprise, standardized unexpected earnings (SUE), and relative strength.

Unexpected earnings (earnings surprise)

earnings surprise = reported EPS − expected EPS

Usually scaled by a measure of the variability of analysts' EPS forecasts. Economic rationale: positive surprises may lead to persistent positive abnormal returns (the market underreacts and keeps drifting up as the news is digested).

Standardized unexpected earnings (SUE)

SUE = earnings surprise / standard deviation of earnings surprise

A given size of forecast error is more meaningful the smaller the size of historical forecast errors — SUE puts the surprise in context of how "surprising" surprises normally are for that stock. Economic rationale: persistence or reversal patterns may exist in stock returns.

Relative strength indicators

Compare a stock's price or return performance during a given period with its own historical performance or with a group of peer stocks. Economic rationale: patterns of persistence or reversal may exist in returns — and these patterns may depend on the length of an investor's time horizon.

IndicatorCompares…Economic rationale
Earnings surprisereported EPS vs. expected EPSpositive surprises → persistent positive abnormal returns
SUEearnings surprise vs. its own historical volatilitypersistence/reversal patterns in returns
Relative strengtha stock's own price/return performance over time, or vs. peerspersistence/reversal, possibly horizon-dependent

LOS 20.rMODULE 20.4

Measures of Central Tendency for a Group of Multiples

Explain the use of the arithmetic mean, harmonic mean, weighted harmonic mean, and median to describe a group of multiples.

A key insight: the P/E of a stock index or portfolio is NOT equal to the simple mean or weighted mean of its constituent P/Es. It is best calculated as the weighted harmonic mean P/E.

weighted harmonic mean = 1 / Σ(wᵢ/Xᵢ)
Worked example — 2-stock portfolio

Stock 1: price $10, EPS $1 (P/E = 10). Stock 2: price $16, EPS $2 (P/E = 8). Portfolio holds one share of each.

TRUE portfolio P/E = total price / total earnings = (10+16) / (1+2) = 26/3 = 8.67×
Four ways to average the same two P/Es (10 and 8) — only one matches the true portfolio P/E of 8.67
MeasureCalculationResult
Arithmetic mean(10+8)/29.00
Weighted arithmetic mean (by price weight: 10/26, 16/26)(10/26)×10 + (16/26)×88.76
Harmonic mean (equal-weighted)2 / [(1/10)+(1/8)]8.89
Weighted harmonic mean (price-weighted)1 / [(10/26)(1/10)+(16/26)(1/8)]8.67 ✓

💡 The weighted harmonic mean exactly equals the true portfolio P/E (26/3 = 8.67) — this is not a coincidence, it's a mathematical identity. This is why it's the theoretically correct way to average a group of multiples.
